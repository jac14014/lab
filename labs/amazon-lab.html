Scientific, Institutional, Legal and Operational Architecture for Acting Before Systemic Reversibility Is Lost
The c-ECO Institute develops an integrated framework for identifying, interpreting and governing systemic risk before critical transitions become irreversible. The c-ECO Framework connects Threshold Dynamics Research (TDR), the Threshold Function Protocol (TFP), scientific calibration, data and evidence governance, Living Labs, Fellowship formation, institutional governance, and legal and contractual mechanisms within a single operational architecture. Its central proposition is straightforward: systemic governance should become operational while meaningful corrective capacity still exists.
Executive Vision

Ecological, financial, infrastructural, technological and social systems often exhibit measurable signals before severe instability, cascading failure or irreversible degradation occurs.

Modern observation systems increasingly detect these signals. The persistent challenge is institutional: scientific recognition does not automatically produce timely governance, financial, contractual or operational response.

Recognition exists.
Coordination is fragmented.
Activation often comes too late.

c-ECO is designed to address this recognition–action gap by linking certified evidence to calibrated thresholds, prudential classification, defined governance triggers, institutional authority and auditable response pathways.

Scientific Foundations, Distributed Intelligence, Interdisciplinary Integration and Emerging Legal Research
The c-ECO Framework is grounded in the convergence of multiple established scientific, institutional and legal research traditions. Its foundations include complex-systems science, critical-transition research, resilience theory, Earth-system science, planetary boundaries, social–ecological systems, network science, critical-infrastructure interdependency, financial systemic-risk research, artificial-intelligence governance, Science and Technology Studies, institutional theory, Earth-system governance, Earth System Law and selected posthumanist and more-than-human perspectives. The framework brings these traditions together around one central question: How can institutions identify, interpret and respond to systemic deterioration while meaningful reversibility still exists?
1. Scientific and Epistemic Status of the Framework

The c-ECO Framework distinguishes between established scientific or scholarly foundations and framework-specific hypotheses, methods and legal mechanisms.

Research on critical transitions, resilience, Earth-system dynamics, planetary boundaries, network cascades, infrastructure interdependency and systemic financial risk provides established scientific foundations.

Institutional theory, Science and Technology Studies, Earth System Governance, Earth System Law, Indigenous and local knowledge research and selected philosophical traditions provide additional institutional, epistemological and interpretive foundations.

By contrast, constructs including Reversibility Liquidity (Lr), the SIL 19×19 architecture, the integrated P / ΔV / σ / Lr methodology, SPS / TRS / RLS, Trajectory Illegality and the legal mechanisms proposed by c-ECO remain framework-specific propositions requiring validation.

Established knowledge provides the foundations.
c-ECO provides an integrative architecture and a research agenda.

This distinction is central to the methodological credibility of the framework. Existing literature should not be represented as validating a c-ECO-specific construct unless that construct has itself been independently tested.

2. Complex Systems, Critical Transitions and Early Warning

Complex systems may exhibit non-linear behavior, feedback effects, regime shifts, critical transitions and abrupt changes in state.

A system that remains apparently functional may nevertheless be moving toward a condition in which recovery becomes increasingly difficult.

Research on critical transitions has demonstrated that some systems may display measurable changes before major state shifts occur, including patterns associated with declining resilience.

These signals do not provide universal or deterministic prediction. They provide a scientific basis for investigating whether deteriorating dynamics can sometimes be detected before a critical transition becomes irreversible.

The scientific problem is not simply to identify collapse. It is to understand the dynamics that precede it.

Scientific foundations: Holling (1973); Scheffer et al. (2009); Lenton et al. (2019).

3. Resilience, Adaptive Capacity and Reversibility

Resilience research examines the capacity of systems to absorb disturbance, adapt, reorganize and maintain essential functions under changing conditions.

This perspective is central to c-ECO because current performance and remaining corrective capacity are not equivalent.

A system may continue operating while its ability to recover, adapt or redirect itself is progressively being depleted.

The c-ECO concept of Reversibility Liquidity (Lr) builds on this broader resilience tradition but is not itself an established resilience metric.

Lr is proposed as a way to estimate the practical capacity still available to stabilize, redirect, repair or restore a deteriorating system trajectory.

Research requirement: Reversibility Liquidity must be empirically calibrated for different systems and cannot be assumed to have the same meaning across all nineteen SIL domains.

Scientific foundations: Holling (1973); Folke (2006).

4. Earth-System Science and the Safe Operating Space

Earth-system science demonstrates that climate, biosphere integrity, freshwater, biogeochemical cycles and other planetary processes operate through interacting feedbacks rather than as isolated environmental variables.

The planetary-boundaries literature introduced the concept of a safe operating space for humanity based on biophysical processes whose destabilization may increase the risk of large-scale Earth-system change.

The framework was initially proposed in 2009, substantially updated in 2015 and reassessed again in 2023.

The 2023 assessment reported that six of nine planetary boundaries were transgressed, reinforcing the importance of examining not merely static boundary positions but the direction and evolution of system conditions.

A boundary is not only a location. Its governance significance depends on trajectory.

Scientific foundations: Rockström et al. (2009); Steffen et al. (2015); Richardson et al. (2023).

Two systems at the same measured position may imply radically different governance conditions if one is stabilizing while the other is rapidly accelerating toward a critical boundary.

6. Coupled Social–Ecological Systems

Ecological systems cannot be separated cleanly from institutions, economies, technology, infrastructure and human behavior.

Research on social–ecological systems emphasizes adaptation, institutional fit, scale, learning and interaction between environmental dynamics and social organization.

Oran Young's work on fit, interplay and scale is especially relevant to the c-ECO proposition that governance structures must correspond to the scale and dynamics of the systems being governed.

This provides part of the intellectual basis for using Living Labs as real-world environments in which technical dynamics and institutional responses can be examined together.

Scientific and institutional foundations: Folke (2006); Young (2002).

7. Interdependent Networks and Cascading Risk

Modern societies depend on interconnected ecological, technological, financial and institutional systems.

Electricity depends on water and digital infrastructure; telecommunications depend on electricity; health systems depend on energy, logistics and communications; finance depends on functioning infrastructure and supply chains.

Research in network science and infrastructure resilience shows that interdependence can amplify disturbances and produce cascading failures across systems that might otherwise appear individually resilient.

The unit of systemic analysis is therefore not only the individual sector. It is the sector embedded within a network of dependencies.

Scientific foundations: Rinaldi, Peerenboom & Kelly (2001); Buldyrev et al. (2010); Helbing (2013).

10. Financial Systemic Risk

Environmental and infrastructural disturbances can propagate through credit, insurance, portfolios, guarantees, asset values and financial markets.

Financial-system research has increasingly used concepts from ecology, network theory and complexity science to understand contagion and systemic instability.

Haldane and May explicitly examined banking systems through an ecological-system analogy, while Battiston and collaborators argued for greater use of complexity theory in financial regulation.

These traditions support the c-ECO decision to treat finance as a system embedded within ecological, infrastructural and institutional networks.

Scientific foundations: Haldane & May (2011); Battiston et al. (2016).

11. Epistemic Pluralism, Co-Production and Multiple Knowledge Systems

Systemic governance depends not only on the quantity of available data, but also on how knowledge is produced, validated, interpreted and incorporated into institutions.

Research in Science and Technology Studies (STS) has shown that scientific knowledge and institutional order are not entirely independent.

The production, interpretation and use of evidence are shaped by measurement systems, institutional practices, expert communities, legal mandates and governance arrangements.

For c-ECO, this means that no single observation system should be assumed to exhaust all knowledge relevant to a complex systemic condition.

Epistemic plurality does not mean that all knowledge claims carry equal evidentiary weight.

It means that relevant knowledge may originate from different scientific, institutional and territorial sources, and that those sources require explicit rules for provenance, validation, uncertainty and authorized use.

Primary foundations: Jasanoff (2004); Berkes (2018); IPBES (2022).

11.1 Co-Production of Knowledge and Institutional Order

The concept of co-production provides an important foundation for understanding why scientific evidence cannot be separated entirely from the institutional structures through which it becomes actionable.

Measurement standards, data classifications, regulatory thresholds, expert procedures and decision rules shape the way systemic conditions are represented.

Scientific findings simultaneously influence the design of institutions and governance mechanisms.

12. Indigenous, Local and Long-Duration Ecological Knowledge

In many territories, ecological and systemic change is observed through knowledge traditions extending far beyond the duration of modern instrumental monitoring.

Traditional ecological knowledge and Indigenous knowledge systems may contain long-duration observations of ecological cycles, species behavior, hydrology, seasonal variability, landscape change and disturbance patterns.

Research in resource management and international biodiversity assessment has demonstrated that such knowledge can contribute meaningfully to environmental understanding when incorporated through appropriate methods, consent, provenance and validation procedures.

The c-ECO framework does not treat Indigenous or local knowledge as an informal substitute for scientific evidence.

It treats it as a distinct knowledge system whose relevance, context, ownership, uncertainty and permitted use must be governed explicitly.

The IPBES methodological architecture is especially relevant because it provides an intergovernmental precedent for recognizing multiple values and knowledge systems within formal scientific assessment processes.

Knowledge-system foundations: Berkes (2018); IPBES (2022).

13. Posthumanist and More-than-Human Perspectives

The broader question of whether governance frameworks are excessively human-centered has also been explored in posthumanist, more-than-human and relational scholarship.

These traditions are relevant to c-ECO at the level of epistemic orientation.

They challenge the assumption that only explicitly human representations of a system should count as meaningful sources of systemic knowledge.

They are not used by c-ECO as empirical validation of TFP variables, SIL architecture, legal triggers or operational thresholds.

Posthumanist scholarship informs the framework's epistemic posture. It does not constitute empirical validation of its operational mechanisms.

Within the c-ECO architecture, this perspective supports attention to ecological relationships, material feedbacks, technological systems and non-human processes without assigning them autonomous legal sovereignty.

Interpretive context: Haraway (2016); Latour (2005); Braidotti (2013).

17. Trajectory Illegality as an Emerging Legal Research Question

Once risk is understood dynamically, an important legal problem appears.

What happens when an activity remains formally lawful today, but its monitored and reasonably foreseeable trajectory is progressively destroying the conditions required for continued systemic viability?

The c-ECO Framework describes this proposed condition as Trajectory Illegality.

Trajectory Illegality is not presented as an established doctrine of contemporary law.

It is a c-ECO legal research proposition arising from the interaction between dynamic systems science and legal governance.

A scientific model may identify trajectory and reversibility. It cannot by itself determine illegality.

That transition requires legal theory, competent authority, due process and appropriately designed legal or contractual mechanisms.

18. Earth-System Governance and Earth System Law

The governance problem addressed by c-ECO is part of a broader scholarly debate concerning whether institutions and law are adequately configured for interconnected Earth-system change.

Earth System Governance research examines governance across scales, institutions and planetary processes.

Earth System Law develops the corresponding legal question: how should law respond when ecological and Earth-system dynamics cannot be meaningfully governed through isolated sectors or conventional jurisdictional boundaries?

This literature does not validate c-ECO's specific legal mechanisms.

It establishes a broader intellectual foundation for investigating them.

Institutional and legal foundations: Biermann (2014); Kotzé & Kim (2019); Kotzé et al. (2022).

19. Beyond Traditional Legal Subjectivity

Legal scholarship has previously questioned whether nature must remain merely an object of human legal relations.

Christopher Stone's classic argument on legal standing for natural objects opened an important line of inquiry concerning legal subjectivity, natural systems and institutional representation.

c-ECO takes a different but related operational position: planetary dynamics do not necessarily need to acquire legal personhood before becoming legally relevant.

c-ECO does not require nature to become a legal person before planetary dynamics become legally relevant.

The research question is whether certified changes in material system conditions may legitimately influence the continued enforceability, authorization or governance of human-created legal relationships.

Legal foundation: Stone (1972).

20. Emerging c-ECO Legal Research Mechanisms

The scientific architecture creates legal questions for which conventional ex-post mechanisms may be insufficient.

c-ECO therefore proposes a family of legal research mechanisms.

These should be understood as propositions for doctrinal, comparative, contractual, judicial and institutional development , not as universally established legal doctrines.

21. Precaution, False Positives and Legal Restraint

Pre-threshold intervention also creates risks.

A governance system that acts earlier can potentially intervene on incomplete evidence, generate false positives, restrict legitimate activity or transfer excessive authority to technical institutions.

For that reason, c-ECO's legal research cannot rely exclusively on arguments favoring precaution.

It must engage directly with critiques of precautionary governance.

Cass Sunstein's analysis is relevant because it demonstrates how precautionary rules can become internally inconsistent, overinclusive or incapable of distinguishing competing risks.

Earlier intervention requires stronger evidence governance, not weaker procedural safeguards.

Pre-threshold governance must therefore develop together with uncertainty treatment, proportionality, review, appeal mechanisms, transparency and institutional accountability.

Critical legal counterpoint: Sunstein (2005).

22. Critical Infrastructure and System Continuity

Critical infrastructure offers one of the clearest examples of why interdependency must be treated scientifically.

Energy, water, communications, transport, digital infrastructure, public health and emergency systems can fail through dependency relationships rather than through isolated component failure.

Rinaldi, Peerenboom and Kelly established a foundational framework for analysing critical-infrastructure interdependencies.

Later network research demonstrated how interconnected systems can experience cascading failures.

These foundations directly inform the logic underlying SIL cross-sector dependency mapping.

Scientific foundations: Rinaldi, Peerenboom & Kelly (2001); Buldyrev et al. (2010).

23. Institutional Fit, Interplay and Scale

Systemic governance is not determined only by scientific capability.

Institutions operate through mandates, jurisdictions, budgets, legal authority, political incentives and different decision horizons.

Young's work on fit, interplay and scale shows that governance effectiveness depends partly on whether institutional structures correspond to the characteristics and scale of the systems they seek to govern.

This is directly relevant to c-ECO because systemic trajectories can cross sectors, territories and institutional mandates faster than conventional governance structures can coordinate.

Institutional foundation: Young (2002).

1. Purpose of TDR

Threshold Dynamics Research is the scientific research program dedicated to the identification, classification, modeling, and validation of signals that emerge before complex systems cross critical thresholds.

Its central purpose is to move systemic risk governance from retrospective diagnosis toward anticipatory recognition.

TDR asks: what can be known before collapse becomes visible?

15. TFP Engine & Variable Processing

The Threshold Function Protocol (TFP) is the methodological engine through which certified evidence is translated into prudentially relevant operational states.

The TFP does not treat risk as a single static number. It evaluates where a system is positioned, how quickly it is moving, how uncertain the evidence is, and how much capacity remains to reverse or absorb the observed trajectory.

σ

Represents the remaining capacity of a system to absorb disturbance, adapt, recover or return toward an acceptable operating state.

The Systemic Coordination Gap

Why Existing Risk Systems Remain Fragmented

The law, governance, infrastructure systems, scientific institutions and financial markets frequently see fragments rather than systems.

Scientific, financial, legal, infrastructural and political institutions operate through different mandates, data structures, decision authorities and timelines.

The result is a coordination failure: important risk information can exist without a shared mechanism capable of translating signals across systems and converting them into synchronized institutional decisions.

5. Four Structural Gaps
Gap Description System Effect
Detection Gap Early-warning systems operate separately across scientific and sectoral domains. Signals may be detected without cross-domain interpretation.
Integration Gap No common mechanism consistently translates scientific evidence into systemic, financial, contractual and regulatory implications. Knowledge exists without a corresponding operational pathway.
Coordination Gap Institutions frequently depend on ad hoc negotiations when crises emerge. Response delays increase precisely when speed is most important.
Legitimacy Gap Global systems may lack local anchoring while local solutions may lack scalable institutional pathways. Solutions become either disconnected from territory or difficult to scale.

3. Urban Resilience Challenge

Manaus faces recurrent urban flooding generated by the interaction of intense rainfall, Rio Negro backwater effects, highly sealed urban micro-basins, constrained drainage capacity, vulnerable settlements, and interconnected urban infrastructure systems.

The Rio Negro reached 29.98 metres at the Port of Manaus on 1 June 2021, the highest level recorded in 119 years. Flooding in the Igarapé do Quarenta basin can affect drainage systems, mobility, access to health and education, housing, sanitation, and other essential urban services.
